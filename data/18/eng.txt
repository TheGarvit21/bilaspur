Sunnud To Rajah Maha Chand of Bilaspur, Dated 6th March 1815



Whereas Rajah Maha Chand of Belaspore has, with sincerity of heart, professed obedience and submission to the British Government, and become a dependant of the Honorable Company, and has cast off all connection with the Goorkha State; therefore, in conformity with the tenor of the Proclamation issued under the authority of His Excellency the Governor-General on the 17th of October 1814, the Rajah is hereby confirmed in the possession of the lands of his ancient territory of Kyloor, actually occupied by him on this side of the river Sutledge, subject to the following conditions:- He shall never openly or secretly ally himself with the Goorkha State, or with any enemy of the Honorable Company, but remaining steadfast in the path of obedience and submission to the orders of the British Government, shall at all times be prepared, with the force which he may have to render useful service to the British troops, providing supplies of grain and furnishing beggars (for the conveyance of burdens), and generally performing whatever may be entrusted to his charge. He shall ever be ready to obey such orders as may be signified to him at the present period, for which may be given to him at any future time, more especially on the occasion of any British force being sent against an enemy in that quarter, when he shall not fail to discharge to the utmost of his ability the obligations of fidelity and attachment to the British Government. Exclusive of the stipulations above mentioned, the British Government, in its liberality and favour, will not require from the Rajah any tribute or pecuniary indemnification of any kind. And in the event of a peace between the British Government and the Goorkha State, provided the Rajah shall nothing contrary to the conditions of protection as affecting the Rajah have rendered faithful service, the British Government engages that shall be listened to by the British Government. Moreover, the terms of the replies to the Rajah's requests, bearing the signature of MajorGeneral Ochterlony, and dated on the 18th of February 1815, are approved and ratified by the Governor-General. It becomes the duty of the Rajah, therefore, that being firmly fixed and established in his Raj, he set his mind at rest on that point, and divesting himself of all apprehension, devote his time to the promotion of the happiness and comfort of his subjects, and consider this as a valid Sunnud for his country. British Paramountcy Established



After the Gorkha war was over, Ochterlony held a conference of the hill chiefs at Plasia in Nalagarh. These chiefs were restored the territories occupied by them before the arrival of the Gorkhas. The connection between powerful rulers, whose control over a weak neighbouring ruler (feudatory) though quite nominal, was severed. However, owing to the difficulty of supervising the whole of the protected hill area, the smaller principalities were placed under the general surveillance of the greater chiefs. Thus, Bushehr became overlord of Delath and Khaneti, Jubbal that of Rawingarh and Dhadi, and Keonthal those of Koti, Madhan, Ghund and Theog. Later on, Ratesh also became the fifth feudatory of the Keonthal state. Yet, other states such as Kumharsain, Balsan, Mangal and Dhami, which hitherto had been petty lordships, were allowed separate chiefships and granted independent sanads. As a result, after becoming the paramount power the British redrew the map of the cisSutlej hill states. In place of the eighteenth-century Barah Thakurain (12 Lordships), their off-shoots and feudatories and the Athara Tbakurains (18 Lordships), totalling 28 states were formed which collectively came to be known as the 'Protected Hill States of Punjab'. After the development of Simla, these states were christened as the Simla Hill States. These states are listed here in the order in which their chiefs sat in durbar: Bilaspur (Kehloor), Bushehr with feudatories Delath and Khaneti; Nalagarh (Hindur), Keonthal with five Zaildars (Koti, Theog, Madhan, Ghund and Ratesh), Baghal, Baghat, Jubbal, Kumharsain, Bhaji, Mahlog, Balsan, Dhami, Kuthar, Kunihar, Mangal, Beja, Darkoti, Tharoch, Sangri, Rawin and Dhadi. Besides these states, there were other hill states namely Mandi, Suket and Chamba, which were designated as Punjab HillStates. For centuries they had been constantly at war over petty issues. Now, an unbroken peace was rendered and mutual wars became a thing of the past; and ultimately, a sense of unity and nationhood started creeping in. Simla district was carved out of these states. In order to have effective control over this hilly area, the British retained a few separate localities situated many miles apart from each other as military posts. In the upper hills, Rawin along with Rawingarh fort, Kotgarh also known as Sandoch that contained forts of Hattu, Silajan and Baji, were taken possession of immediately after the expulsion of the Gorkhas. In the lower hills, the strategically important Subathu was retained. The fort of Malaun and six villages surrounding it were taken from the Raja of Nalagarh, who was compensated elsewhere. From the ruler of Baghat, who had not helped the British in the Gorkha war, Bharoli was taken, which consisted of three parganas. By the acquisition of Malaun and Bharoli, which were very important military positions, the Company secured undisputed command of the lower hills. Strategically important forts, both in the upper and lower hills, were garrisoned with Nasiri (friendly) Gorkha battalions, which were raised from amongst the defeated Gorkha troops. While making territorial settlement, the Company's government further punished the hill rulers who did not support the new divisions and sold their territories to its allies. For example, the Rana of Bhagat was deprived of five parganas of his state yielding an annual revenue of Rs. 14,000 and were sold to the ruler of Patiala for rupees 1,30,000. The latter, who had helped the British with troops and supplies, was further given more territories. As the Keonthal chief had not helped the British, his state was dismembered anda portion comprising nine parganas, yielding about Rs. 21,000 annually, were sold to the Raja of Patiala for Rs. 1.50 lakh. In this way, the Patiala ruler occupied territories in the hills, for which he was required to pay a nazrana of Rs. 2,80,000 to the British government. This led to the introduction of the Patiala factor in the Simla Hill States, which influenced certain developments in the region. In 1816, the British also retained a tract of land belonging to the Keonthal state. It was Simla, the future summer capital of the Government of India. Lieutenant Ross, assistant political agent for the hill states, erected the first English house at Simla, a wooden cottage with a thatched roof in 1819. The place was further developed during the period of Captain C.P. Kennedy, who succeeded Ross, and stayed at Simla until 1835. Lord Amherst, governor-general (1823-28), visited Simla in 1826. and within few years, it became a favourite resort with the Englishmen, both officials and non-officials. After the annexation of the Punjab in 1849, Lord Dalhousie, governor-general (1848-56), came here regularly, though his favourite resort was Chini (Kalpa) in the Bushehr state. By mid-nineteenth century, Simla grew into a small town of a hundred houses, with a British population of three hundred and twenty during the summer. Successive governors-general visited Simla with increasing regularity during the hot season. In 1864, it became the summer capital of the Government of India. Simla was the regular headquarters of the commander-in-chief prior to that of the governor-general. The latter's secretariat remained in Simla for about seven months whereas several of the army headquarters offices remained in Simla all the year round. The Punjab government first came to Simla town in 1871. The town, thus became the capital of the 'greatest empire in the world'. In addition to Simla and the aforementioned places, the British also took possession of Kotkhai in 1828, where its ruler and the wazis had let loose a reign of terror and oppression on the people. With the passage of time, more places and tracts such as Jutogh, Solan, Daghshai, Kasauli and Sanawar, etc., were also acquired by the British government. The entire tract known as Simla district had an area of 101 square miles, and was perhaps the smallest district in India. From 1849, i.e., the time of the constitution of the Board for the administration for the affairs of the Punjab, up to the year 1859, the Simla hill states were controlled by the superintendent in direct communication with, and in subordination to, the Board of Administration or the chief commissioner. In January 1859, after the creation of the Simla district, its deputy commissioner superintended the administration of the Simla hill states and was also designated as the superintendent, hill states. In his civil capacity as deputy commissioner, he was subordinate in all departments to the commissioner of the Ambala Division. In this capacity he was also civil judge, bound to decide all suits of Rs. 1,000 and upwards, as district magistrate he was also head of the police, and he attended all the Municipal work. But in his political capacity as superintendent hill states, he was independent, responsible only to the head of the Punjab government. As a result of this arrangement, in the civil department, the deputy commissioner was bound to conform to fixed rules of business, but in the political department the discipline was not as stringent. The consequence of mixing these two systems was that instead of becoming regular and methodical in the discharge of his political duties, the deputy commissioner of Simla became lax and irregular in all the departments. Sir John Lawrence, the chief commissioner of the Punjab, took serious view of it that the above double system was anomalous in theory and defective in practice. He declared that with effect from February 19, 1859, the deputy commissioner of Simla was subordinate to the commissioner, whose office was then located at Ambala. The system of the political control of the Simla hill states by the Punjab government through the instrumentality of the superintendent, hillstates continued till 1921. After the Act of 1919, there was a need to bring the larger and important Indian states into direct relations with the Government of India. In 1921, the Punjab States Agency was established and the Punjab government surrendered its relations with Bilaspur and Sirmaur. After the Act of 1935, a further stage was reached in the process of transferring Indian states from political relationship with local governments to political relations with the Government of India. In the Punjab a new agency, the Punjab Hill States Agency, with headquarters at Simla was formed with effect from October 1, 1936. Under this scheme, the Simla hill states and Kalsia, which were under Punjab government, Tehri Garhwal that was under the government of United Provinces, and Sirmaur and Bilaspur which were under the agent to the governor-general, Punjab states were incorporated in the new agency. From the details above, it is clear that there evolved what may be called a five-tier system and four-tier system for formulating and controlling the British policy and relations in respect of the Simla hill states. From 1859 till March 1889, it was the five-tier system which worked, i.e., there was the superintendent, hill states, the commissioner, the Punjab government, the Government of India, and the home government superintendent in London. From April 1889, the superintendent was placed directly under the control of the Punjab government and henceforth it became a four-tier system, which continued till 1947. The superintendent of the hill states enjoyed vast powers and his position in the Simla hill states was quite enviable. The various chiefs kept their vakils in constant attendance on the superintendent and he conveyed his views through them to their rulers and wazirs and they, in turn, arranged audiences with other chiefs when necessary. He exercised residuary jurisdiction, which was defined as jurisdiction exercised in cases that were beyond the powers of a chief. This jurisdiction was also



exercisable in these states in any such case where political considerations precluded the exercise of a chief's jurisdiction. Under this jurisdiction, the superintendent was also entitled to try a member of a ruling chief's family, particularly the heir-apparent, for a serious criminal offence. Another important matter in which this jurisdiction was exercised w the preservation of the Hindustan-Tibet road as well as other hill roads. Capital sentences passed by the chiefs required confirmation by the superintendent before they could be carried out. He also heard appeals against the hill chief's decisions and if he felt that a particular case was not being dealt with fairly in the state courts, he could get the case transferred to his own court. In a nutshell, in addition to enjoying his powers and position as deputy commissioner of the Simla district, the superintendent, as local representative of the paramount power, enjoyed enormous powers and occupied a prominent position in the Simla hill states.
